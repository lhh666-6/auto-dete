\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The experiments expose two different notions of performance. Predictive performance asks how often the recognizer is correct. Governance performance asks whether an incorrect or irrelevant machine output can bypass an accountable decision. The HOG--SVM result shows that predictive performance can be substantially improved by replacing the current template matcher. The fault matrix shows that the authority boundary remains useful even when prediction is strong, because rare errors, model drift, irrelevant retrieval, and generative suggestions still require a controlled destination.

The selected threshold illustrates the practical tension in selective prediction. A 1\% calibration risk target led to 11.19\% evaluation coverage and 88.81\% routing. This operating point is inappropriate if manual review capacity is scarce, even though its accepted subset was error-free in the sample. A deployment should therefore choose a threshold through an explicit cost model that values an unreviewed error, a review minute, and delayed processing. Calibration should be repeated after changes in forms, printers, cameras, or recognizers. The descriptive Wilson lower bound further cautions that 292 correct accepted rows do not imply perfect accuracy; dependence among perturbations makes formal uncertainty wider than the row-level calculation suggests.

The architecture is model-agnostic. A stronger classifier can supply candidates and confidence scores; a conformal or learned selector can replace the margin rule; and a different retrieval or language model can produce assistance. Each remains outside the fact-writing interface. This modularity separates model procurement from authority design and creates a stable audit question: which service can call the fact-transition operation?

The five fault injections should be viewed as executable design assertions. They provide stronger evidence than a diagram alone because they exercise concrete services and stored state. They are weaker than formal verification, adversarial security assessment, or coverage of every UI and migration path. Future assurance could add property-based state-machine tests, database privilege separation, signed audit events, and independent red-team scenarios.

The most important next empirical study is not another synthetic recognition benchmark. It is a blinded, double-entered corpus of real forms, with adjudicated ground truth and subgroup reporting by template, device, writing style, and degradation. A second study should randomize review interfaces---recognition only, retrieval-assisted, and AI-assisted---and measure review time, correction accuracy, automation bias, and inappropriate suggestion acceptance. Those studies would test whether the architecture improves decisions rather than merely constraining software transitions.
