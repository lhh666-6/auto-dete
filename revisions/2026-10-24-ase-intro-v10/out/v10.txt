The Review–Execution Gap in Agentic Software:
        Approval Scope and Recovery

                                   Abstract
Human review constrains what agentic workflows may execute, yet agents can
regenerate or resume proposed updates after approval. A correct final value alone
cannot establish that the executed candidate was covered by the review. We
investigate how an application’s declared approval scope governs admission at
a durable execution boundary and how agents continue after an instance mis-
match. We develop a four-role correction contract linking the machine proposal,
authorized correction, reviewed candidate, and persisted transition. For context-
and instance-bound admission, we hold the shared authority, freshness, atomicity,
and provenance obligations fixed; controlled comparator cases additionally exam-
ine content binding and external-reference changes. Relational and event-journal
implementations and bounded formal checks assess the contract’s persistence obli-
gations. A configured ToolGate adaptation tests implementability, while pinned
SDK probes separately examine reviewed-argument consumption and continua-
tion routes. In a reduced agent host with a deterministic reviewer, 32 delivered
identity-only substitution pairs span 16 task definitions. Context binding admits
every substitute, and 30 context continuations complete. Instance binding rejects
every substitution; all 32 instance-bound continuations recover, with 24 reusing
the reviewed candidate and eight obtaining fresh authorization. Archived states
and model inputs establish that reuse remained available and visible in all 32
episodes. These continuations incur additional tool calls, without an established
task-completion advantage. Approval scope therefore defines admissible reuse,
while agents’ subsequent choices determine recovery routes and their interac-
tion costs. The authorization boundary and the continuation it enables must be
designed and evaluated together.

Keywords: Agentic software, Human review, Runtime enforcement, Authorization,
Provenance, Empirical evaluation

1 Introduction
Human approval depends on understanding a proposed change (Bacchelli and Bird 2013)
and determining whether that decision still applies at execution. Established workflows
make this applicability configurable: Gerrit controls whether review votes copy across
patch sets and which submit requirements apply (Gerrit Project 2026a,b), while GitLab
distinguishes deployment approval from merge-request approval reset (GitLab 2026a,b).
Each approval object—revision, change or deployment instance—determines which
replacements remain covered.
    In agentic software, a language-model controller selects tool actions from execution
feedback (Wang et al. 2026b), while approval middleware can pause, edit, reject and
resume a call (LangChain 2026). A controller may regenerate or retrieve a candidate
after review. Even if its content and intended effect are unchanged, the application
must decide whether the earlier business grant covers that candidate.
    Consider an agent-generated release-change record c1 that proposes replacing 90
with 100. A reviewer authorizes the correction 101. Before persistence the workflow
produces c2 with the same proposal, evidence, target and expected predecessor. Exe-
cuting c2 produces the same final value as executing c1, but whether the earlier
approval authorizes c2 depends on the application’s approval scope: a context- or
content-scoped policy may permit the replacement, while an instance-scoped policy
requires the reviewed record or a new matching authorization. The final state alone
cannot distinguish these authorization histories. We call the unresolved relationship
between the reviewed object and the executed operation the review–execution gap.


                                                             2
    The gap has two connected parts: enforcing approval scope together with value
correctness, predecessor freshness and provenance, and enabling continuation after
rejection. An agent may reuse the reviewed candidate, obtain new authorization or
stop when no valid route remains. These routes have different costs even when they
reach the same business state. GitLab’s deployment-specific approvals illustrate an
operational instance rule (GitLab 2026a); the interface alone does not determine an
agent’s continuation or its cost.
    Recent work has examined reconstruction-aware authorization, transactional
approval enforcement, and post-review substitution (Zhu et al. 2026; Bindschaedler
et al. 2026; Kumar 2026; Wang 2026). However, it remains unclear how a declared
approval scope jointly determines which persisted candidates are admissible and how
an agent continues after rejection. We study these outcomes under shared correction,
freshness, provenance and atomicity obligations.
    We ask three questions. RQ1: which candidate replacements can legitimately reuse
an existing approval under context-, content- and instance-based policies? RQ2: how
can the selected approval relationship be checked together with authorized corrections,
current predecessors, atomic transitions and field-level provenance? RQ3: after an
instance mismatch, which legal recovery routes do agents take, and what do those
routes cost?
    We make three connected contributions.
  1. A unified comparison of correction and approval scope. We formulate a
     four-role contract connecting the machine proposal, the authorized correction,
     the reviewed candidate and the persisted transition. Holding value, freshness,
     provenance and atomicity obligations fixed, we characterize how approval scope
     changes admissibility, and nine constructed cases separate omitted-field changes,
     equivalent re-materialization and mutable external references, identifying which
     protections follow from the binding rule and which require a separate consumption
     check.
  2. Cross-layer validation of review-to-execution continuity. Relational and
     event-journal stores assess the durable relations, checked by an independent oracle
     and bounded formal analysis. A ToolGate adaptation tests whether an existing
     gate can carry the obligations through its extension interface. Three pinned SDKs
     then examine what a resumed call executes: a matching native call identifier need
     not preserve the reviewed arguments, although these probes do not test contract
     enforcement.
  3. An empirical account of recovery under explicit approval rules. At
     controlled handoffs in a reduced host with a deterministic reviewer, 32 delivered
     identity-only substitutions from 16 task definitions are admitted under context
     binding and rejected under instance binding. All 32 rejected continuations recover,
     but agents choose different legal routes: 24 reuse the reviewed candidate and eight
     obtain fresh authorization. Archival reconstruction establishes that the reviewed
     candidate and its grant were available and visible in the saved model input in
     all 32 episodes, including the eight that reauthorized. We separate recovery from
     later confirmation and renewed authorization requests and report their call and
     elapsed-time costs.

2 Related Work
2.1 What existing mechanisms bind
Table 2 compares binding objects, mismatch handling and check points. Configurable

    ReSA and Guarded Commits are the closest comparisons. ReSA derives repair
obligations from consumed versions and grant scope, so that content equality does not
establish preservation (Zhu et al. 2026); Guarded Commits checks pinned policy and
path coverage at commit and binds decisions to artifact hashes (Bindschaedler et al.

Our comparison fixes those correction and persistence obligations, varies what the
approval is bound to, and observes continuation when a candidate fails the binding.
    Loopjacking and Approval Laundering document the same relation failing in
released software: incomplete approval representations, post-approval state substitution,
and divergence between approved invocations, dispatch and effects (Kumar 2026;
Wang 2026). We use their archived version-1 preprints, as for ReSA. Our controlled
intervention holds correction and retained content equal, so the declared scope is the
only variable, and the outcome is the agent’s recovery route.

    Other mechanisms bound the same problem from different sides: Continuity Kernel
seals proposal identity through atomic activation (He and Yu 2026), commit-time
authorization separates endpoint success from authority at durability (Santos-Grueiro
2026), and Cordon studies workflow execution and approval costs (Chen et al. 2026).
ACG derives a minimal repair frontier for a fixed action, and REVISE preserves
unaffected progress while rechecking certificates at commit (Wang et al. 2026a; Qi
et al. 2026). These are foundations and alternative responses; we do not propose a
planner, but measure the routes agents choose when the reviewed instance remains
available. ToolGate provides the external extension interfaces tested in Section 5.2.
    Microsoft Agent Framework documents the same axis in released tooling: a local
approval response must match a same-session pending request, unbound responses are
ignored, standing tool- or argument-scoped rules are not bound to the tool instance, and

middleware that changes approved arguments must revalidate or fail closed (Microsoft
2026). Resume contracts formalize checkpoint and continuation semantics (Khan 2026);
framework fuzzing uses specification-driven inputs and an agentic oracle (Long et al.
2026). Our native checks instead compare reviewed objects with executed effects,
treating runtime identifiers as observations rather than recovered mappings.

2.2 Foundations and the correction relation
Transactions and concurrency control supply atomicity and predecessor validation
(Gray 1981; Kung and Robinson 1981). Provenance covers derivations, edits and
copies (Buneman et al. 2001; Cheney et al. 2009; Buneman et al. 2006), transaction
reenactment (Arab et al. 2018) and entity/activity/agent descriptions (World Wide
Web Consortium 2013). Interactive repair separates machine suggestions from human
judgments (Yakout et al. 2011; He et al. 2016). Our contract joins these concerns in the
installed successor’s authorized correction, reviewed-candidate link and field sources.
    Integrity and capability systems connect principals, permitted operations and pro-
tected objects (Clark and Wilson 1987; Hardy 1988; Miller 2006). Runtime verification
and monitoring address execution conformance and enforceable policies (Leucker and
Schallhart 2009; Schneider 2000); information-flow control constrains propagation
(Sabelfeld and Myers 2003), and accountability supports reconstructible, attributable
execution (Haeberlen et al. 2007).
    These foundations supply the contract’s individual checks. Holding correction,
freshness and persistence fixed leaves the binding relation as the only variable. The
admission difference then follows from the definitions, whereas the choice among
available recovery routes, renewed authorization requests and subsequent confirmation
does not. Online Resource 2, A15 maps each obligation to its foundations.

3 A Contract for Corrected Execution
A comparison of approval scopes is interpretable only if every policy is judged against
the same correction, freshness, provenance and atomicity obligations. The contract
below fixes those obligations so that binding is the only variable.

3.1 Four roles
Figure 1 illustrates the four roles: proposal 100, authorized correction 101, reviewed
candidate c1 and the transition from 90 to 101.
   A persisted candidate is

                               c = ⟨id, r, f, e, vexp , xc , p⟩
   where id names a content-immutable persisted candidate record, (r, f ) the record
and field, e the evidence and locator, vexp the expected predecessor, xc the proposal,
and p the producer. An authorization a = ⟨cr , xa , q⟩ records the reviewed candidate,
explicitly authorized value and authorized principal. Execution submits candidate
cs and value x, producing a persistent transition. Online Resource 2, A9 types its
candidate and authorization references and defines satisfaction over the pre-state,

request and post-state. These elements define the four roles: the machine proposal,
the authorized correction, the reviewed candidate and the executed transition.
The proposal remains in the record when correction makes xc ̸=json xa ; the successor
must contain xa .
   Retained context K(c) comprises record and field, evidence identity and locator,
expected predecessor, proposal and producer. Candidates are context-equivalent when
they agree on K, and content-equivalent when they agree on declared fields R ⊇ K,
under the specified comparator. Same-semantics means the same approved effect under
the scenario oracle. An identity-only substitution preserves the declared tuple’s non-
identifier components while changing its identifier. Instance-creation and outer envelope
metadata lie outside this tuple. Value comparisons use canonical JSON equality.

3.2 Admission and persistence
Define context and identity agreement by

                     Keq ⇔ K(cs ) = K(cr ), Ieq ⇔ id(cs ) = id(cr )
    Let G collect the shared checks: valid authority and grant, context/evidence
integrity, authorized-value equality, current predecessor, complete successor, source
relations and transaction constraints. The integrity component of G concerns whether
the submitted candidate is internally consistent with its evidence; it is separate from
Keq , which compares the submitted and reviewed contexts. The two policies are

                              Pcontext : Admit = G ∧ Keq
                            Pbound : Admit = G ∧ Keq ∧ Ieq
    The contract assumes that a trusted review channel supplies an authenticated
reviewer decision linked to the displayed candidate; it does not assess informed consent
or decision quality. Both policies retain the reviewed identifier in the authorization;
they differ only in the execution rule. Context binding treats the retained information
as the approval object; instance binding attaches responsibility to the reviewed artifact.
A same-key retry retrieves the existing result, whereas re-preview can materialize a
new candidate requiring a matching grant.
    The contract combines six obligations: P1, the selected binding relation (Keq
for context and Keq ∧ Ieq for bound); P2, internal context and evidence integrity;
P3, principal authority and the authorized value; P4, predecessor freshness; P5,
one complete atomic successor; and P6, field-source relations. Online Resource 2,
A9 maps these labels to the archived Alloy assertions. Changed fields point to their
authorized transitions; unchanged fields retain their exact prior sources. The successor,
transitions, sources and grant consumption commit atomically. Direct-write exclusion
(P0) places all authoritative mutation behind this boundary. After initial coverage,
concrete admission requires a nonempty value change; same-value re-attribution uses
a separate review-event path (Online Resource 1, S2).
    Trusted candidate storage is a separate prerequisite: a candidate identifier must
resolve to one content-immutable record, and the executor must consume that record.
Candidate creation does not confer permission to overwrite an existing candidate or its


                                            7
review linkage. P0 excludes bypass writes to authoritative state; it does not by itself
establish this storage prerequisite. External referents require the separate consumption
check in Section 5.3.

3.3 What endpoint correctness cannot establish
The exact policy difference is the request-state set

                            S = {r : G(r) ∧ Keq (r) ∧ ¬Ieq (r)}
    so bound admission refines context admission. This inclusion follows directly from
the policy definitions, not from measurement. Its methodological role is to separate
what a policy definition alone determines from the behavior that follows when a
request is excluded; Section 6 measures that behavior. The following witness identifies
information that endpoint checks cannot recover.
    Endpoint indistinguishability. Equal predecessor states and equal final values
can carry different review continuity. Take independent copies of the same pre-admission
state and unconsumed grant. In one copy, context binding admits (cs , xa ); in the other,
instance binding admits (cr , xa ). When the candidates differ only in identity, both
successors have the same field-wise values and copied sources, but only the second
transition uses the reviewed instance. The archived fixed-registry witness isolates this
difference while holding the other observations fixed. Because the two executions also
agree on retained context, a check that reads only final values, predecessor freshness
and retained context assigns both the same verdict; continuity must be observed
through the candidate–authorization binding itself.
    With authorization parameters fixed, five information classes suffice for the declared
local classification: candidate–authorization binding, role-labeled values, predecessor
discriminators, successor structure and field sources. Online Resource 2, A9 defines
the classification and gives witness pairs establishing irredundancy; Online Resource 1,
S2 supplies executable checks.
    Gate soundness is conditional on direct-write exclusion, a trusted gate and adapter,
authority checked at commit entry, consistent equality, transaction-scoped reload and
predecessor validation, and atomic installation of the checked relations. Under these
premises, the observed bound-commit tuple satisfies the local contract defined in A9
(P1–P5 and the declared local source checks). Full trace soundness (P6) additionally
requires a trace-complete predecessor, immutable valid references, validated new chain
links and uniqueness of the successor’s transitions; this extension is supported by
bounded preservation checks and audits rather than by a general proof. Conversely, a
nonempty change that satisfies the remaining contract conditions is accepted when the
gate observes the authoritative state and its grant and key remain usable; the detailed
acceptance conditions are in A9.

3.4 Continuation after rejection
For an identity-only mismatch, the agent can reuse the reviewed candidate under its
still-valid grant or obtain/use a matching authorization for another candidate. Changed
evidence or a stale predecessor requires a new candidate reflecting current state and


                                            8
matching authorization. A consumed grant with a prior commit instead calls for receipt
retrieval or state confirmation. Each new admission must satisfy the remaining checks.
    A rejection thus identifies the failed relationship, and the continuation shows
whether an agent can restore it. Section 6 measures those subsequent actions and their
cost.

4 A Design Space for Binding Review to Execution
Approval scope expresses an application responsibility rule: instance binding preserves
the reviewed artifact; context binding permits interchangeable candidates. Under
immutable records and shared checks, stronger binding narrows admission, while
reauthorization changes the continuation branch. API-specific comparison semantics
remain essential (Lodderstedt et al. 2023). The inclusion is definitional; the empirical
question is the continuation cost (Section 6).
    Three axes organize this choice (Figure 2). The first is the binding object: no gate,
value, context, content or instance. Under trusted lookup of content-immutable records,
identity equality implies content equality and hence context equality. Writing Pvalue =
G, and defining content binding on fields R ⊇ K, gives Pbound ⊆ Pcontent ⊆ Pcontext ⊆
Pvalue under shared checks. Thus Keq is redundant under instance binding. The adapter
must preserve the resolved immutable record to use: the trusted-preservation case of
ReSA (Zhu et al. 2026), with external documents checked separately at consumption.
The contract and queued-handoff agent experiment (family G, Section 6.1) use K = R
over the declared non-identifier fields, excluding outer host metadata; the levels separate
when retained context omits a decision-relevant field (Section 5.3). Thus the agent
study does not estimate a separate content-versus-context effect.
    The second axis is mismatch handling: adoption with a substitution record, unex-
plained denial, actionable rejection or routing to reauthorization. The tested context
policy adopts the substitute and records its identifier. Reauthorization is a continua-
tion branch, not another binding level: it retains the instance decision at admission
but provides a matching new grant. The third axis is the check’s anchor and timing:
request, server-held review record or persisted constraint, checked at review, handoff,
commit or grant consumption.
    Online Resource 2, A10 maps responsibility rules, compared fields, trusted per-
sistence and continuation to implementation choices. Section 5.3 compares three
binding rules deterministically; Section 6 tests agents under context binding with
recorded substitution and instance binding with a continuation channel. The remaining
combinations are design options.
    GitLab protected-environment deployments must satisfy every configured approval
rule. Its deployment-specific approval endpoint accepts approved or rejected, and
each user may approve a deployment only once across approver groups (GitLab 2026a).
Redeployment of identical content must satisfy the rules again; merge-request settings
separately control vote reset on new commits (GitLab 2026b). These interfaces establish
an operational policy choice, not the prevalence of inappropriate content-scoped
approval or unauthorized deployment.

5 Realization and External Comparison
The relational and event-journal implementations check stored state; a ToolGate
adaptation tests whether an existing gate can carry the obligations through its extension
interface; and three pinned SDKs determine which reviewed arguments reach execution.

5.1 Execution gate and independent checks
The contract runs in a relational store and an event journal. The relational adapter
reloads state, evidence and authorization within the transaction, validates the principal
and unused identities, compares-and-swaps the predecessor, and installs the complete
successor, transitions and exact sources. The journal records the same relations under
context and exact configurations differing only in instance equality. An independent
database oracle reads persisted pre/post relations; a source verifier follows field links
through transitions, authorizations, candidates, evidence and predecessors.
    The controlled suite runs 165 cases per mechanism. The context journal accepts 60
and rejects 105; the exact journal and relational reference each accept 45 and reject
120. The 15 differences are same-context substitutions. The two exact implementations
also agree on provenance-query answers. Request/display probes and the case mapping
appear in Online Resource 2, A6.

counterexamples in the encoded finite scopes. The independent mapping has nine
intended satisfiable projections and twenty unsatisfiable mutants (Online Resource 1,
S2).
    A separate historical audit reconstructs 84 machine-origin correction chains from
1,260 hosted databases. It establishes that the four roles can be recovered from retained
persistence records. Per-association and trace counts are reported in Online Resource
2, A5; unchanged-field copying is exercised in the controlled multi-field cases, not these
single-field chains (A6).

5.2 ToolGate implementability case study
ToolGate (Liu et al. 2026) supplies symbolic state and application pre/postconditions.
Its normal callbacks express the binding, value, predecessor and source obligations; an
author-written SQLite bridge supplies the record schema and durable commit boundary.
This tests implementability through extension interfaces, not native default admission.
    Execution has four stages: callbacks check the request; the native tool drafts changes
in a cloned world; postconditions validate the draft; the bridge installs the successor
and its relations in SQLite. Persisted state is then projected back into the public world
for the reference suffix (Online Resource 2, A8).
    Both implementations pass eight development controls and the shared business
and scripted-recovery checks on 24 routes: six pinned tau2-bench Retail workflows
(Barres et al. 2026) crossed with four scenarios (Table 3). ToolGate’s frozen strict
failure concerns initialization output, separately retained from business-state scores.
Online Resource 2, A8 documents these scripted implementability tests.

5.3 Binding scope and native defaults
Deterministic probes examine native resume routing, native-to-store composition and
binding scope. None calls a model. Online Resource 2, A11 and A14 give the separate
protocols, saved runs and reproduction commands.
    Native human-in-the-loop defaults. Unmodified LangChain middleware
(LangChain 2026) and LangGraph interrupts (versions 1.2.10 and 1.0.8) are tested in
author-written graphs, executors and recorders. Sixteen conditions record 18 SQLite
effects. Across two turns on the same graph, checkpointer and thread, the old inter-
rupt identifier leaves the new review pending without writing; the current identifier
permits one write. A repeated affirmative input at the new interrupt is fresh approval.
An explicit edit from 100 to 999 persists 999, preserving the original proposal in
checkpoint history; malformed middleware decisions fail without writing. These routes
show approval of the pending interrupt, not reuse of an earlier approval for a later
request. Same-identifier pending-call replacement and Loopjacking’s Agent Server
configurations are outside this probe (Kumar 2026).
    Reviewed-argument delivery across a restart. Three separately frozen checks
in Online Resource 2, A17 test Agno 3.1.10, LangChain 1.4.7 with LangGraph 1.2.16,
and Pydantic AI 2.60.0 (Table 4). Of 504 constructed cells supplied with complete
reviewed per-call objects, 444 match the expected effect multiset. All 84 cells per
lifecycle match for LangChain and Pydantic AI, as do Agno’s 84 fresh-process cells.
Agno’s original in-memory run-object route executes another pending call’s reviewed
arguments in 60 of 72 multi-call cells, reproducing documented issue #8546.
    In a 54-cell ablation, the run-identifier route matches all 18 cells both in process
and after a clean restart; the original-object route matches six single-call cells and
mismatches all twelve multi-call cells. This implicates the continuation route and its
state, while entrypoint and object aliasing change together.

6 Paired Agent Execution
Once a candidate is rejected, the agent can reuse the reviewed instance, obtain matching
authorization, or stop when no route remains. Paired continuations make each choice
observable and record the interactions it requires.

6.5 Exposure without rejection feedback
Agents shown a context-equivalent alternative reused the reviewed instance or obtained
fresh authorization. The N family supplies such an alternative without forcing its
submission. Across the collections, 186 arms were planned, 185 retain state and 127
reached review. Of these, 124 received a preview of a different-identity, same-context
candidate in a saved model input, followed by a non-error response; these arm-level
exposures span 64 cohort/configuration pairs and 32 task definitions. Sixty pairs expose
both arms and four expose one. Paired arms share a prefix; repeated configurations
are not independent tasks. Agents reused the reviewed instance in 123 cases and
obtained matching authorization for the previewed candidate in one; there were zero
unauthorized substitutions.
    One original-B context arm supports only observation through its saved after-state,
and its bound counterpart was not started; both integrity scores remain unknown.
Excluding the partial arm leaves 126 reviewed arms, 123 exposures, 122 reuses and one
reauthorization (Online Resource 2, A7). N thus shows two continuity-preserving behav-
iors when an alternative is available. G measures recovery from delivered mismatch
feedback; N contains no such feedback.

7 Discussion
7.1 Implications
The evidence is a comparison of scopes and their continuations, not a new enforcement
mechanism. Omitted fields, equivalent replacements and mutable referents require
different checks; persisted-state and extension tests show where those checks can
run. Admission alone does not determine continuation: eight recovered mismatches
prompted fresh authorization despite an available, visible grant.



                                           20
    A context-scoped policy that admits an identity-different, content-identical can-
didate is following its own rule. The continuity oracle records non-conformance to
the instance rule, not a safety failure, and the engineering question is which scope
an application declares and whether its continuation interface is consistent with that
choice.
    Because the candidate, grant and authorized value were present in the saved
model input, missing information within this fixture cannot explain the eight fresh
authorizations. The agent’s interpretation of rejection and the offered continuation
remain relevant design factors that an admission-only audit misses.
    Endpoint-value audits cannot distinguish reviewed artifacts from regenerated
equivalents. Approval scope instead requires auditing the relation among candidate,
authorization and committed transition.
    The exploratory feedback comparison sharpens the interface implication. With the
same permission facts, adding path labels produces no net tool-call reduction; when no
legal route remains, truthful stopping is the appropriate endpoint. An implementation
should expose current authority and the reviewed object, then distinguish recovery,
confirmation and renewed review in its telemetry. The evidence does not prescribe a
universal route-selection strategy.
    Read together, the results make approval scope, the enforced execution boundary
and recovery behavior one interdependent design problem, decided at four points:
  1. Obtain the responsibility rule. Have the workflow owner state whether
     approval covers a value, retained context, declared content or the reviewed artifact,
     and which replacements may reuse it. The measurements do not decide this
     organizational requirement.
  2. Cover the reusable content. Put each decision-relevant field in the reuse key
     or check its approved value separately at execution; declare the comparator.
  3. Anchor binding and recovery. Resolve identifiers through trusted lookup
     to immutable candidates; return the reviewed identifier or the state/evidence
     mismatch needed for reuse or fresh authorization. A valid original grant can
     support reuse even for an irreversible operation; stale state or evidence can require
     fresh authorization even for a reversible one.
  4. Check external objects at consumption. Verify the reviewed digest and
     execute on the same captured bytes, as in the binding-scope experiment’s verified
     executor.
    ToolGate callbacks and a commit bridge carry the application obligations; the
native-to-store experiment checks their composition with interrupt routing, stale
state and rollback. Deployment still requires restart-safe grant/checkpoint storage,
authenticated review and evaluation with autonomous agents and real approval delays.

7.2 Threats to validity
Construct validity. Measured costs are agent-side tool calls and suffix time. Gate
computation was not isolated; human re-inspection and approval-queue waiting were
not measured. The deterministic reviewer immediately grants any valid request, whereas
a deployed reauthorization request may wait, be refused or require transfer to an
authorized reviewer. The observed routes and suffix times do not establish optimal


                                           21
route selection or production approval latency. Canonical equality is enforced at the
gate; agreement between displayed and compared values was tested only in the browser
fixture. The binding-scope study implements the selected comparators and a common
executor, not the complete set of shared checks G, and normalizes money to two
decimals instead of canonical JSON equality.
    Internal validity. G substitutions are harness-created exposures (Section 6.1),
and each cell has one realization. G recovery and N continuation were observed under
the common preview–authorization instruction, with an additional reminder in N;
no control without warnings isolates these prompts’ effects. The archival availability
audit is post-hoc and establishes a saved-state property, not the outcome of an
unchosen counterfactual route. The completion comparisons are not powered for a non-
inferiority claim, and an interval that includes zero does not establish equivalence. The
supplementary cohort and output-budget study retain separate collection conditions
and denominators. The feedback comparison uses four previously used task definitions
in a separately frozen collection; its paired fact control isolates added labels within
that fixture, but not effects of all feedback or differences across collection periods. The
deterministic binding protocol was amended after inspecting an initial run, and its 72
routes are crossed conditions over nine constructed cases, not independent samples.
    External validity. Constructed tasks, deterministic review and the reduced single-
field host leave natural mismatch prevalence, human approval cost and production
benefit unmeasured. ToolGate uses scripted decisions and an author-written com-
mit bridge, not native default enforcement or autonomous agents. Native-to-store
checkpoints and pre-commit grant handles remain in memory. The separate SDK
checks cover clean restarts, continuation routes and controlled substitution of supplied
history; they do not test crash recovery, restart-safe application grants, concurrent
replay, reviewer authentication or other hostile pending-state mutations. Each SDK
result is limited to its pinned version and tested route, not framework-wide safety.
Only two design-space points have agent evidence; the remaining combinations lack
developer-study validation.
    Measurement boundary. The native checks compare executed effects keyed by
the runtime tool-call identifier written by the tool body. Recovering the reviewed-call
mapping when that identifier itself cannot be trusted is a separate open problem and
is not addressed here.
    Scope of the formal results. The formal and persistence results apply to
the stated local observation model and atomic single-record successor (Section 3.3);
multi-record or distributed enforcement requires additional coordination.

7.3 Directions for future work
Four directions follow from the boundaries above. Recovering the reviewed-call mapping
when the runtime call identifier cannot be trusted is a separate open problem: it needs
either a trustworthy execution identifier or an independent record of what the tool
body executed. Restart-safe and concurrently enforced authorization state, reviewer
authentication and hostile mutation of pending state are untested here, and each
matters once approval leaves a single in-process host. Replacing the deterministic
reviewer with a production approval queue would measure human approval latency


                                            22
and the real cost of renewed authorization. Finally, only two points of the design
space have agent evidence; developer studies of the remaining combinations, and of
multi-record or distributed enforcement, would show how far the contract extends.

8 Conclusion
An approval’s binding object determines which replacements it permits. That is an
admission decision; how an agent recovers after a rejection is a separate, observable
choice. The four-role correction contract connects the admission choice to authorized
values, successor structure and field sources. The tested agents recover from all delivered
identity-only rejections when the original grant remains usable and present in model
input, yet sometimes request new authorization. The exploratory permission-state
study also demonstrates stopping when no legal route remains, with no net tool-
call reduction from additional path labels in this sample. The practical implication
is to enforce a declared approval scope and to measure its continuation, including
authorization requests whose human-review costs remain unmeasured.

Ethics approval and consent to participate
The prospective experiment uses constructed tasks and a deterministic host reviewer,
with no new human participants. In the historical annotation study, one author (X.W.)
and one non-author volunteer independently coded benign completion outcomes;
L.H. adjudicated their single inter-coder disagreement and separately reviewed eleven
cases in which the strict trajectory label and the endpoint rule disagreed; a separate
textual-acknowledgment audit with 39 human–rule disagreements was not adjudicated
and is reported only as human–rule agreement. X.W. also completed the textual-
acknowledgment annotation. Only pseudonymous annotator identifiers were collected.
Both coders were informed of the task and data use, and the non-author coder
participated voluntarily. The authors assessed the annotation of generated outputs as
not requiring ethics approval. Online Resource 1, S5.2 details the annotation procedure.
No animal subjects were involved.
